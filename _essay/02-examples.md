As the bicentennial of the Lewis and Clark Expedition
approaches, identifying the explorers' route through Idaho
becomes an important and controversial task. Nearly
thirty-eight years ago Congress recognized the historical
importance of the expedition's route, and the Lewis
and Clark Trail Commission was established on October
6, 19641 as a five-year temporary body with advisory
powers. Congress asked the Commission to identify,
mark, and keep publicly available the route traversed by
Captains Meriwether Lewis and William Clark on their
1804-06 travels from the mouth of Wood River, Illinois,
to the Pacific Ocean. As a result, "in 1978 the roughly
3,700 mile long Lewis and Clark Trail...was designated
a National Historic Trail."

Lewis and Clark's journey over the Lolo Trail and the
Bitterroot Mountains in September of 1805 was one of
the most difficult passages of the expedition. This portion
of the trail covers approximately 150 miles from Lolo,
Montana, to Orofino, Idaho. At the Weippe Prairie the
trail forks, the 1805 fork westward goes to Orofino, and
the 1806 eastward return route goes from Kamiah to the
Weippe Prairie. This portion of the Lewis and Clark Trail
crosses the Lolo National Forest, the Clearwater National
Forest (where approximately 96 miles of the trail are
located), private lands, and Idaho state lands. The trail
through the Clearwater National Forest is spectacular,
traveling through pristine high mountain peaks, ridges,
and meadows.

The Lewis and Clark Trail in Idaho is the most valuable
portion of the trail, in many places the trail has
not been disturbed by man for almost 200 years. In the
past, a number of researchers, including Ralph Space,
a former supervisor of the Clearwater National Forest,
have attempted to positively identify the trail, but it has
proved to be a difficult task. The goal of identifying the
trail has not been realized· and many sections of the Lewis
and Clark route have not been properly identified. This
needs to be done so that visitors can experience the same
difficult challenge that faced the Lewis and Clark Party.
The visitor will want to walk where "they" walked, walk
on unaltered trail tread straight up the mountains and
across the ridges. Proper identification will also enable
the United States Forest Service to protect not only the
Lewis and Clark Trail, but also prehistoric and historic
sites and travel networks. Furthermore, trail studies will
help anthropologists to understand the Lewis and Clark
trail as it relates to cultural migration and diffusion by
Native Americans.

The Lewis and Clark Trail is only one trail in the "Lolo
Trail System," which was defined by the Clearwater National
Forest in 1985 as a corridor containing the following trails:
the Lewis and Clark Trail, the Lolo Trail (or Northern Nez
Perce Trail), the Bird-Truax Trail (minimal grading of the
Lolo Trail, 1866), the Nee-Me-Poo (new name for the Lolo
Trail, 1877 Nez Perce War route), and the primitive Lolo
Truck Road (Road 500). This trail corridor on the Clearwater
National Forest varies from 2 miles wide to less than ¼ mile
wide and encompasses most of the five trails, which follow
roughly the same route over much of the forest, and became
a National Historic Landmark in 1960

Ralph Space, retired forest supervisor of the Clearwater
National Forest and author of several works on the
Lolo Trail, defined the historical trails on the Clearwater
National Forest as the Lolo Trail (also defined as the
Northern Nez Perce Trail), the Bird-Truax reconstruction4
and the Forest Service reconstruction of the Lolo Trail.
Space also further defined the Lolo Trail as one of the
two main routes of travel for the Nez Perce to the buffalo
herds in Montana and Wyoming.5 Space acknowledged
the historic importance of the Lolo Trail as "the oldest
route across the Continental United States traveled by
white man. Lewis and Clark first traveled it in 1805 and
their journey alone makes quite a story."6
The oldest locations of the historic trails can be
determined by examining historic maps. George B.
Nicholson's 1867 map of the Lolo Trail identifies the trail
(also defined as the Lou-Lou or Lo-Lo Trail) as coming
from the Weippe Prairie to Musselshell Meadows, over
Snowy Summit to Rocky Ridge and along the divide to
Willow Ridge. At this juncture Lewis and Clark, coming
up out of Hungery Creek, joined the Lolo Trail, and
continued eastward on the trail into Montana.

The 1879 map of the Nez Perce Indian Campaign by the U.S. Army
Department of the Columbia indicates the same location
as Nicholson's map.8 Forest Service maps (beginning
with Lieberg's 1898 map of the Bitterroot Reserve and
subsequent maps by the Clearwater National Forest up
to 1942) also define the Lolo Trail as the route from the
Weippe Prairie to Musselshell Meadows, over Snowy
Summit and Rocky Ridge to Willow Ridge and on to
Montana, on the historically correct Lolo Trail.

In 1866 the Lolo Trail was rebuilt with minimal
grading by Bird-Truax. From 1902 through 1907, the
Forest Service also rerouted the Lolo Trail. In the 1930s,
the Forest Service with the Civilian Conservation Corps
program built the 500 Road which follows closely the
original Lolo Trail. In the 1960s, the Forest Service began
intense logging of the historic trails, which has continued
to the present day.

However, the Forest Service has recognized the
importance of identifying the trail, and has continued
to conduct research and fieldwork over the years. This
work led up to the field reconnaissance of 1983 and 1984
which was designed to locate trail tread and map the approximate
trail system across the Clearwater National
Forest. 1° Forest Service archaeologist Milo McLeod accomplished
similar work on the Lolo National Forest in
1977 and 1978.11 Accordingly, when the Forest Service
defined the Lolo Trail as five trails it also concluded the
trail was "probably never one trail prior to 1866, but was
most likely a braided trail system covering a major ridge
or drainage which sought out the path of least resistance."
Thus, the braided trail myth was created. This researcher
found that one reason for the existence of braided trails
is the Forest Service's reconstruction and relocation of
old historic trails. Ralph Space documented the braided
trails created by the Forest Service in its relocation of the
Lolo Trail in Siberia Creek.

moose), or by Forest Service reconstruction of segments
