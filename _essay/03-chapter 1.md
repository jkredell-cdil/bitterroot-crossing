The Lewis and Clark Trail is without a doubt the
most important historic trail in the Pacific Northwest.
Nez Perce Indians in prehistoric and historic times traveled
the Lolo Trail from the Clearwater and Snake River
basins to the buffalo hunting grounds in Montana. Pi LuYe
Kin described a typical trip in 1870 like this: "Under
the leadership of Looking Glass and Shorn Head the Nez
Perces started from the plains of Oyaip in June, 1870, one
hundred and thirty tipis strong-from twelve hundred and
fifty to fifteen hundred people. About two months were
consumed in the journey to the buffalo country.',24
This 150-mile route also played an important part in
the historic Lewis and Clark expedition going west in
1805 and again on their return journey in 1806. While
most of Lewis and Clark's travels overland occurred in
Montana (where major highways and roads parallel most
of the Trail), the Clearwater National Forest in Idaho has
the longest number of miles of primitive unroaded Lewis
and Clark Trail than anywhere else in the nation. It is the
roughest piece of trail Lewis and Clark crossed, and it is
still undeveloped in many places. The Idaho portion of
the Lewis and Clark trail also has been the least modified.
Portions of the trail in Fish and Hungery Creeks remain
as they were almost two hundred years ago, as reported
by The Lewiston Morning Tribune in 1984, stating that
"during 1984 a field reconnaissance was made of the
roughly twelve miles of the Hungery Creek drainage. This
section of the Lewis and Clark Trail is widely considered
to be the least disturbed section of the entire 3,700 mile
route and the one most closely approximating conditions
similar to those viewed by Lewis and Clark."25 We have
the opportunity to preserve the old route, mostly as it
was when the Corp of Discovery struggled over the steep
mountains on their journey west.

The Weippe Prairie area played a key role in the Lewis
and Clark expedition, where the friendly Nez Perce helped
the weary explorers after completing the most difficult
part of the entire journey-the crossing of the Bitterroot
Mountains. After meeting the Nez Perce, Clark hurried
back to the Lewis party on September 22nd
, 1805, and
described their condition: " ... walked up to the 2nd village
where I found Capt Lewis & the party Encamped, much
fatigued, & hungery, much rejoiced to find somethin::§
to eate of which They appeared to partake plentifully.''
The Nez Perce befriended and fed the white men, and
took them into their lodges. The lodges on the Weippe
Prairie numbered approximately 200 in 1805; Clark's
map indicates five village sites on the prairie. The Weippe
Prairie has been an important camas gathering area for
the Nez Perce Indian Tribe for centuries. The exact
meaning of the name Oyayp, Oyipe or Weippe has been
