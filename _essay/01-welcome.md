The geographic theme of this book is the beautiful mountainous region of Idaho stretching between Lolo Pass and
-the Clearwater River valley near modem day Orofino. This landscape was used by the Nez Perce people for thousands
of years before Lewis and Clark reached the area in 1805. While modified by humans in the succeeding two hundred
years, the Clearwater Mountains retain many scenic and historical treasures little changed by modem development.
Within the context of the Lewis and Clark Bicentennial observations, the authors of this book seek to examine the
historical resources of this region, the trail remnants in particular. In doing so, they have applied a fine mix of archival
research, cartographic and air photo sleuthing, all combined with a thorough and remarkable knowledge of the ground
itself. The Eastmans have also been successful in collecting much of the oral history of land management in the corridor
traveled by the Nez Perce Indians and subsequently, Lewis and Clark.

This book contains a fine bibliography of the many applicable written resources needed to understand the history
and subsequent management of the trail corridor. The bibliography is especially useful in listing the important, but hard
to locate, "gray" literature relating to the region.

Also included is the most thorough cartobibliography ever compiled for the Clearwater Mountains, including maps
from collections throughout the region, and from the National Archives as well. These maps, coupled with careful use
of early air photos, offer a great deal of useful and important information on the human modification of this region's
great historical resources. No other scholars of the route of Lewis and Clark in Idaho have been as successful in this
important map research as have been the Eastmans.

This book makes a useful contribution to our understanding of trail distances and the abilities of early travelers to
cross a difficult landscape. It also offers some valuable insights into the types and locations of trails used by the Nez
Perce and Salish people who crossed the area in search of buffalo, fish, and other trade goods. All of these contributions
to our knowledge are informed by the thousands of hours spent in the field by the authors. The result is a valuable and
important contribution to the scholarship of the adventures of Lewis and Clark. There are also some useful suggestions
for the preservation of the remaining historical resources.

Dennis Baird
